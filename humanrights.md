# ⚠️ The Mental Health and Wellbeing Act 2022: Is Victoria's System Allowing Torture to Happen?

> ## My contention
>
> I believe Victoria's **Mental Health and Wellbeing Act 2022** creates and maintains powers that can enable coercive psychiatric intervention.
>
> My concern is that when those powers are misused, poorly supervised, or used alongside conduct causing severe physical or psychological suffering, the system may **allow treatment to cross the line into cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment, or potentially torture**.
>
> This is an allegation and legal argument that should be tested against the evidence and the applicable law. It is not presented here as a court finding.

---

## 🧠 Why This Matters

Mental-health legislation operates in an area where the State can exercise enormous power over an individual.

The central question I am asking is:

> **What happens when powers intended for treatment and protection are instead used in circumstances that cause severe and prolonged suffering?**

Calling something "treatment" does not automatically answer whether the way a person was treated was lawful or compatible with their human rights.

# ⚖️ Victoria Already Has a Human-Rights Standard

The **Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities Act 2006 (Vic)** includes:

- **Section 10** — protection from torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment
- **Section 21** — right to liberty and security of the person
- **Section 22** — humane treatment when deprived of liberty

Section 10 is especially important when examining allegations involving medical or psychiatric treatment.

# 🌍 International Human Rights

The prohibition against torture does not disappear simply because the person concerned is a psychiatric patient.

Relevant international standards include:

- **Article 5 — Universal Declaration of Human Rights**
- **Article 7 — International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)**
- **UN Convention Against Torture (CAT)**

The legal definition of torture has specific requirements. Severe suffering alone does not automatically establish torture.

Factors including **purpose, severity, circumstances, responsibility and official involvement** matter.

---

# 🇦🇺 Torture Is Also Addressed by Australian Criminal Law

Australia's **Criminal Code Act 1995 (Cth), Division 274** contains a specific torture offence.

That offence applies where its particular legal requirements are satisfied, including requirements concerning:

- severe physical or mental pain or suffering;
- particular purposes such as punishment, intimidation or coercion; and
- the required connection with a public official or person acting in an official capacity.

# Human Rights Framework

> **Important:** This page describes the legal framework that could apply **if allegations of non-consensual medical procedures, torture, coercion, or other mistreatment were established by evidence**. It does not, by itself, establish that those events occurred.

## ⚖️ Core Prohibitions

International human-rights law provides strong protections against torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment.

Relevant instruments include:

- **Article 5 — Universal Declaration of Human Rights**
- **Article 7 — International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)**
- **UN Convention Against Torture (CAT)**
- **Common Article 3 — Geneva Conventions**, where applicable
- Relevant principles of international law

Article 7 of the ICCPR protects against torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment. It also expressly prohibits medical or scientific experimentation without free consent. The UN Human Rights Committee states that Article 7 protects both physical and mental integrity and permits no derogation, even during a public emergency.

## 🧠 What Is Torture?

Under the **UN Convention Against Torture**, torture has a specific legal definition.

It includes the intentional infliction of **severe physical or mental pain or suffering** for purposes including:

- obtaining information or a confession;
- punishment;
- intimidation;
- coercion; or
- discrimination;

where the required connection to a **public official or person acting in an official capacity** exists.

The Convention also states that exceptional circumstances cannot be used to justify torture.

## 🏥 Medical Treatment and Consent

Medical procedures performed without legally valid consent can raise serious human-rights issues.

Depending on the circumstances, relevant protections may include:

- bodily and mental integrity;
- protection from torture or cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment;
- medical treatment and informed consent;
- liberty and security of the person; and
- protection against arbitrary detention.

Whether a particular medical procedure was lawful depends on matters such as the applicable legislation, the person's capacity, consent, clinical circumstances, and any lawful authority relied upon.

## 🇦🇺 Australian Law

Australian Commonwealth criminal law contains a specific torture offence under:

**Criminal Code Act 1995 (Cth), Division 274**

Section **274.2** addresses conduct inflicting severe physical or mental pain or suffering for specified purposes where the required official-capacity connection exists.

## 🟣 Victorian Human Rights

In Victoria, the **Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities Act 2006 (Vic)** contains several potentially relevant protections.

### Section 10 — Protection from torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment

Section 10 addresses protection from:

- torture;
- cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment; and
- medical or scientific experimentation or treatment without full, free and informed consent.

Other potentially relevant Charter rights include:

- **Section 8** — Recognition and equality before the law
- **Section 9** — Right to life
- **Section 21** — Right to liberty and security of person
- **Section 22** — Humane treatment when deprived of liberty

## 🔎 Applying These Laws to Allegations

If evidence established that a person had:

1. undergone repeated medical procedures without legally valid consent; and
2. subsequently been deliberately subjected to severe physical or psychological suffering;

that evidence would need to be assessed against the precise requirements of these laws.

The legal characterisation could depend on:

- who performed or authorised the conduct;
- whether valid consent existed;
- what legal authority was relied upon;
- the purpose and severity of the alleged conduct;
- whether public officials were involved; and
- the available medical, documentary, forensic and witness evidence.
